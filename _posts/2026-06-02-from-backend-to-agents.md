My path into AI did not start from research taste alone. It started from backend work.

I spent time working on market-data systems and later on production LLM services, and that changed how I think about AI. I became less interested in whether a model can produce an impressive answer once, and more interested in whether the whole system still works when real users show up with real constraints.

That shift matters.

## Backend experience changes how you see AI

When you have spent time around production services, you naturally start asking different questions:

- What happens when latency spikes?
- What breaks when the tool call fails?
- What part of the output can be trusted?
- What assumptions stop holding once the traffic is real?

Those are not always the most glamorous questions, but they are often the ones that decide whether a system is usable.

## Why agents became interesting to me

Agents are where these questions become more visible. Once a system starts making decisions across multiple steps, using tools, retrieving information, or coordinating subtasks, the difference between a demo and a product becomes much sharper.

That is what keeps me interested in this space. I do not just want to build systems that look intelligent. I want to understand how to build systems that stay useful under real conditions.

Right now, that has led me toward multi-agent systems, retrieval, reliability, and long-horizon learning. Over time, I think those threads may connect to broader forms of agentic and possibly physical AI, but the foundation still feels the same to me: strong systems thinking first.
